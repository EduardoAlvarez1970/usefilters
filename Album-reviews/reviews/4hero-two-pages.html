4 Hero

Two Pages (1998)

The third album by the duo formed by Mark “Marc Mac” Clair and Denis “Dego” McFarlane, Two Pages was released in 1998 on Talking Loud. More than just a drum and bass record, this work represents one of the moments when the genre expanded into more organic and musically ambitious territory.

4 Hero, pioneers of jungle and broken beat, partially step away from the strictly programmed paradigm here, instead embracing a more “live” sound. In a genre often dominated by beatboxes and intensive sampling, the duo prioritizes the interaction between real musicians: Luke Parkhouse on drums and Andy Hamill on double bass are fundamental to this sonic construction. String arrangements, percussion sections, and several vocal performances, including the unmistakable Ursula Rucker, complete the picture.

From a production standpoint, the album breathes. There is less surgical editing and more room for performance and improvisation. The double bass solo on the opening track, “Loveless,” sets out the album's principles: organic groove, real dynamics, and drums with jungle DNA, yet played with a jazz sensibility.

Marc & Dego's work, with technical assistance from Leon Mar, alternates between compositions clearly geared toward a “live band” format — rhythm section, string arrangements, and vocals — and moments where the programmed pulse that defined them in the '90s resurfaces. The result is a warm, expansive sound that at times evokes the free jazz of the 1970s.

As the album progresses, its aesthetic direction becomes more firmly established through a palette that, personally, brings to mind Stevie Wonder's output from the first half of the 1970s: that blend of harmonic sophistication, groove, and rhythmic spirituality. There are also echoes of early Jamiroquai: “Escape That” could easily sit alongside tracks from Emergency on Planet Earth.

“Spirits in Transit,” meanwhile, stands out for its level of detail and balance in the production, recalling the work of the most meticulous practitioners of sophisticated pop, where every element occupies its precise place in the spectrum.

Throughout the album, 4 Hero make it clear that they have not abandoned the 808 or their samplers. “Universal Reprise” and other passages show how they integrate programming and sonic manipulation without falling into excessive artificiality. There are filters, delays, and subtle processing techniques, but they always serve the overall sound.

“Golden Age of Life” expands the sonic palette even further: additional percussion, harp arrangements, acoustic and electric piano, backing vocals, and Carol Crosby's lead vocals combine to create one of the album's richest moments in terms of layers and textures. Later on, “Star Chasers” serves as a prelude to “Wishful Thinking,” where they revisit Patrice Rushen with a respectful yet personal approach.

The second half of the album moves into darker territory, with a stronger focus on more technical drum and bass. With “Humans,” the duo approaches the world of meticulously programmed beats also explored by artists such as Roni Size and Goldie. Here, rhythmic engineering takes center stage once again, without sacrificing the musicality that runs throughout the entire project.

Toward the end, “Three Thousand and Five (Edit)” brings the 303 into a rhythmic foundation featuring all the classic elements of D&B, demonstrating that the duo can move naturally between organic sounds and pure electronic production.



Conclusion

Two Pages is an album deeply committed to its aesthetic, yet open in its musical language. Drum and bass may not be everyone's comfort zone, but from the standpoint of production, arrangements, and sonic construction, this is a work that deserves careful exploration.

More than a genre record, it is an example of how to expand an electronic musical language by incorporating real musicians, dynamics, and expressive intent. For those who enjoy analyzing layers, mixing decisions, and rhythmic architecture, there is plenty to discover here.

My rating: 8.5 / 10
